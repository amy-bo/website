Precision fermentation uses microbes, usually genetically modified, to make one particular molecule, which is then purified out of the brew. Insulin and rennet are the best-known examples. Industry estimates put fermentation-made chymosin (the active part of rennet) in most US cheese ([Wikipedia: Chymosin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chymosin)).

The same approach now makes dairy proteins without cows: whey protein (beta-lactoglobulin) and casein, the main protein in milk and cheese. Several have been cleared in the US since Perfect Day in 2020, but no precision-fermented animal protein has yet been approved in the EU or the UK ([FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2024/04/17/Precision-fermentation-dairy-seeks-EFSA-approval-What-we-know-so-far/)).

## Why it isn't our focus

- **Purification:** separating one protein from the brew makes it more complex and expensive than eating the whole microbe, and harder to do at small scale.
- **Feedstock:** the microbes are usually fed sugar, which has to be grown on farmland.
- **Closed strains:** companies that invest in custom strains are unlikely to open source them. Open science projects such as [Real Vegan Cheese](https://www.realvegancheese.org/) and [Open Insulin](https://openinsulin.org) have shown it can be done in the open, and we'd love to see more.
