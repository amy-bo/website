Sustainable protein is protein made without the land, water and emissions that livestock needs. Livestock uses 83% of the world's farmland but supplies only 18% of its calories ([Poore & Nemecek, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaq0216)). There are four main alternatives, each with its own page:

| Form | What it is | Main hurdle | Where it stands (October 2026) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Plants](/sustainable-protein/plants/) | Protein eaten straight from crops, or made into meat-like foods | Still needs farmland, weather and season | Mainstream; plant-based meat sales falling |
| [Precision fermentation](/sustainable-protein/precision-fermentation/) | Microbes make one specific protein (whey, casein, rennet), which is purified out | Purification cost; usually genetically modified strains | Approved in the US; no animal protein approved in the EU or UK yet |
| [Single-cell protein](/sustainable-protein/single-cell-protein/) | The whole microbe is the food: fungi, algae or bacteria | Scale, cost and approvals | Mycoprotein (Quorn) mainstream; protein from hydrogen-oxidising bacteria approved in Singapore and sold in the US |
| [Cultivated meat](/sustainable-protein/cultivated-meat/) | Animal cells grown in bioreactors | Cost of the growth medium; scale | Approved in Singapore, the US and Australia; UK pet food only; banned in some places |

Insects are another option (the EU has authorised four species), but they are still farmed animals and the largest insect farming company went into liquidation in 2025, so we don't cover them here.

## Why we are working on hydrogen-oxidising bacteria

Hydrogen-oxidising bacteria (HOB) are single-cell protein with one big difference: they don't eat crops. Fungi and precision fermentation are usually fed sugar, which has to be grown. HOB make protein from hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide, and hydrogen and oxygen can be made from water and electricity.

- **Land and water:** powered by solar panels, HOB could make about ten times as much protein per hectare as soya, the most efficient protein crop ([Leger et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2015025118)), with about a tenth of its water ([Sillman et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gfs.2019.09.007)). Both are models, not measurements.
- **Anywhere:** no sunlight, soil or season, so protein can be made where crops fail.
- **Simple to process:** the whole cell is the food, so there's no purification step as in precision fermentation. That makes it the best fit for small, local, open source production.

The honest counterpoints:

- **Energy:** HOB need a lot of electricity. Their carbon footprint beats plant protein only when that electricity is low-carbon ([Järviö et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2021.145764)).
- **Digestibility:** the first independent tests on Solein, the only HOB protein on sale, rate its digestibility as moderate in a lab model ([Caliskan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodres.2026.118467)). There are no human digestibility data yet.
- **Processing still matters:** bacterial biomass is high in nucleic acids, which have to be reduced before it is eaten in quantity.
- **Regulation and money:** Solein's EU application has been in since 2021 without an opinion, and two HOB start-ups closed in 2025 after running out of capital.

Some of the studies above include authors from Solar Foods. That is one more reason we think the field needs open data that anyone can check.

## A note on closed source companies

We mention a number of commercial companies in these pages. We support their efforts, and commercial competition has brought many good innovations to market. But good companies and their patents can be bought by less benevolent ones, and shareholder primacy means the world's poorest, in the most challenging environments, may not benefit from the innovations they need most. Open source work like ours benefits everyone, companies included, while making sure the results stay available to all.
