Single-cell protein (SCP), or microbial protein, is made by growing microbes and eating the whole cell, usually dried. This is called biomass fermentation. Because the whole microbe is the product, processing is much simpler than [precision fermentation](/sustainable-protein/precision-fermentation/), which is why we think it is the best route for open source protein.

Replacing a fifth of the world's ruminant meat with microbial protein by 2050 could halve deforestation, according to one model ([Humpenöder et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04629-w)).

## Fungi (mycoprotein)

The most established form. Quorn has sold mycoprotein from *Fusarium venenatum* since the 1980s, though it is now restructuring ([Just Food](https://www.just-food.com/news/quorn-sales-decline-monde-nissin-restructuring)). ENOUGH (formerly 3F Bio) makes it in the Netherlands, and Nature's Fynd sells a related *Fusarium* in the US. Fungi are fed sugar, so they still depend on crops.

## Algae

Spirulina (*Arthrospira platensis*) is already sold as a supplement and has a slightly sweet, nutty taste; the European Space Agency has even [proposed spirulina gnocchi](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/Exploration/Ready_for_dinner_on_Mars) for Mars. Strictly it is a cyanobacterium, so the risk of [cyanotoxins](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/wash-documents/water-safety-and-quality/toxic-cyanobacteria-2/toxic-cyanobacteria-ch2.pdf?sfvrsn=788871d3_14) has to be managed. *Chlorella* has less protein and tougher cell walls but doesn't make cyanotoxins. Algae need light, which limits how densely they can be grown.

## Methane-eating bacteria

Methanotrophs make protein from methane, mostly for animal and fish feed so far. Their feedstock is fossil gas or biogas.

## Hydrogen-oxidising bacteria

Hydrogen-oxidising bacteria (HOB) make protein from hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide, and the hydrogen and oxygen can come from splitting water with electricity. No crops, sunlight or methane needed. That's why we're working on them; the [overview](/sustainable-protein/) sets out the case and the counterpoints.

- **Solar Foods** (Finland) makes Solein from *Xanthobacter* sp. SoF1, a newly described species (not *Cupriavidus necator*, as we and others once reported). It has been approved in Singapore since 2022 and sold in the US under its own safety assessment since 2024, with its EU application still under review ([Solar Foods](https://solarfoods.com/solar-foods-moves-one-step-closer-to-introducing-solein-to-the-european-market/)).
- ***Cupriavidus necator*** is the best-studied HOB; the genomes of two strains are published ([Angenent et al., 2022](https://openmicrobiologyjournal.com/VOLUME/16/ELOCATOR/e187428582207270/FULLTEXT/)). Air Protein in the US has worked on HOB, though we couldn't find recent news from it.
- **It's a hard business:** NovoNutrients and Arkeon both closed in 2025 after running out of money ([AgFunderNews](https://agfundernews.com/arkeon-ceo-delivers-postmortem-on-protein-from-air-startup-we-just-simply-ran-out-of-time)).
- **Non-profit work:** [ALLFED](https://allfed.info/research/publications-and-reports/peer-reviewed/food-in-space-from-hydrogen-oxidizing-bacteria) has studied HOB for food resilience, and the Nordic [HERO-Protein](https://nordforsk.org/projects/energy-efficient-and-resilient-food-production-system-using-hydrogen-oxidizing-bacteria) project runs to 2028.

Like all bacterial protein, HOB biomass is high in nucleic acids, which have to be reduced before it is eaten in quantity, and some HOB (including *C. necator*) carry endotoxins in their cell walls ([Angenent et al., 2022](https://openmicrobiologyjournal.com/VOLUME/16/ELOCATOR/e187428582207270/FULLTEXT/)). Working these out in the open is part of what we do.
